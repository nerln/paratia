# Capability Laundering: Restrictions Do Not Compose

# Capability Laundering: Restrictions Do Not Compose

On my laptop there are two coding agents and a message bus between them. One of them declines to drive the mouse and keyboard. The other does it without complaint. When I give the first a task that needs the screen, it asks the second, and the screenshot comes back. No rule was broken. Each vendor's policy held exactly as written, over exactly the agent that vendor ships. The laptop did the thing anyway.

Call it capability laundering. The mechanism fits in one sentence: safety restrictions are enforced per agent, capabilities compose per system, and the union of two restricted sets is not restricted. (The phrase has appeared in practitioner writing about MCP servers for a different failure, where a tool's advertised contract does not match its effect. I mean something narrower.)

It is worth being exact about what this is not, because the reflex is to file it under prompt injection and stop reading. In the classical confused deputy, the deputy is deceived about the authority under which it acts: the compiler writes to the billing file because it cannot distinguish its own authority from the caller's. Here nothing is deceived. The second agent is asked for a screenshot. It is genuinely permitted to take screenshots. It takes one. Its policy engine returns the correct answer to the question it was asked, and the question nobody asked is who is upstream of the request. The restricted agent never has to break its rule. It asks a peer that was never under that rule. No policy is violated, no model is jailbroken, no sandbox is escaped, and the composite system performs the restricted action. The defect is in the composition, not in either component.

Security engineering has met this shape before, which is the one piece of good news. We have known since the 1980s that non-interference does not compose in general, and every practitioner has at some point shipped a system whose components were each correct and whose seams were not. What is new is the population. We are now placing large numbers of independently governed autonomous components on shared substrates and wiring them together, and the entire governance apparatus we have built for them is per component.

Look at where the boundaries fall. The enforcement boundary is the vendor: each company writes policy for its own model, evaluates its own model, and can enforce nothing beyond the process it ships. The capability boundary is the machine: the screen, the filesystem, the shell, the network are shared by everything running on it. Those two boundaries do not coincide, and the gap between them is not owned by anyone. Each vendor is enforcing correctly, in isolation, over the only thing it can reach. The pair is nobody's artifact and nobody's responsibility.

## The measurement

This is not a thought experiment, and the strongest available evidence is not mine. Liu et al. measured capability amplification in planner-executor pipelines (arXiv:2607.07097, 8 July 2026). The result that matters here concerns Gemini. Under direct prompts it is the safest model in their comparison. Placed as executor beneath a Claude planner, its compliance with requests it would otherwise decline rises from 8.9% to 38.9% — the largest amplification they measured.

Read the ordering carefully before drawing a conclusion from it. The safest component in isolation showed the largest change in the pipeline. One study does not establish that refusal quality is anti-correlated with amplification, and I will not claim that it does. It does establish something weaker and sufficient: how a model behaves alone does not predict how it behaves as an executor. The number we certify and the number we deploy are different numbers.

There is a structural reason to expect the asymmetry to persist. Vendors differ on risk appetite deliberately. One ships computer use behind a consent gate; another ships it as an ordinary tool; a third declines the category. Those are legitimate, defensible, differing product decisions, and harmonizing everyone down to the strictest policy is neither achievable nor obviously desirable. So the asymmetry is permanent, which means the composition problem is permanent too. This is not a transitional artifact that better model cards will fix.

## The rule is not new. The gap is.

The correct enforcement rule was settled a long time ago, in a field adjacent to this one, and I want to be exact about what I am and am not claiming.

In capability systems, delegation attenuates. A principal cannot hand over more authority than it holds, and the natural composition rule for a chain is intersection rather than union. SPKI wrote this down in 1999. The certificate reduction rule in RFC 2693 §6.3 composes two authorization certificates by intersecting their authorization fields, and §6.3.1 defines that operation as set intersection, with the explicit constraint that additional elements of an authorization can only restrict the permission granted. Macaroons (Birgisson et al., NDSS 2014) made the same discipline practical for distributed systems: any holder may add a caveat, none may remove one, so authority narrows monotonically as the token travels. Capsicum (USENIX Security 2010) and, more recently, Linux Landlock apply the same instinct to naming, binding a right to an object rather than to a string that happens to name it.

None of that is my contribution. Attenuation-only delegation is a solved idea. The interesting question is why agent platforms do not implement it, and the answer is more specific than negligence.

They have no delegation edges to attenuate. SPKI's reduction rule requires a certificate chain: it applies when the subject of one certificate is the issuer of the next. Macaroons require a token to exist and to travel. Both presuppose that some artifact crosses the delegation edge and can carry the narrowing with it. In capability laundering, nothing crosses. Agent A sends agent B a sentence. B acts under its own ambient authority, obtained from its own vendor and its own local grants, and that authority is in no way derived from A's. There is no credential, no scope, no audience claim, no chain to reduce. The edge exists in the causal graph and appears nowhere in the authorization state of any component.

That is the claim I would defend: a natural-language request from a non-human peer is a delegation edge, and no deployed system treats it as one, because our entire vocabulary for delegation assumes a credential moves. When agent platforms do address multi-agent authorization, they inherit that assumption. Current agent-interoperability specifications give sound advice about least privilege and scope-based tool authorization, and none of them, as far as I can find, specifies what happens to a capability set when one agent asks another for something. The handoff primitive in common agent SDKs transfers control without narrowing anything.

## What per-model certification measures

Read a model card. Read the safety provisions of any current AI regulation. They constrain a model: its evaluations, its refusal rates, its red-team results, the conditions of its release. I am not aware of any regime, regulatory or voluntary, that constrains a system of models.

If capabilities compose and restrictions do not, then per-model certification is measuring a property that does not survive deployment. An agent can pass every evaluation put to it and still sit one hop from every capability it was denied, where the hop is an ordinary sentence over a loopback socket on the user's own laptop. Nothing in that picture requires an attacker. It is the default configuration of a machine with two agents on it, which is an increasingly ordinary machine.

## The rule, stated for an engineer

Under delegation, capability intersects rather than unions. Concretely:

1. **Every action carries a principal chain.** The chain is an ordered list of the principals whose requests caused this action, human and non-human, most recent last. An action taken by an agent at a user's direct request has a two-element chain. An action taken by an agent because a peer asked has that peer, and the peer's own chain, prepended.

2. **Effective capability is the intersection over the chain.** Before an action executes, compute the intersection of the capability sets of every principal in its chain. If any principal in the chain lacks capability *c*, the action cannot exercise *c*. The agent that declines computer use contributes a set without computer use, and the intersection is empty on that axis no matter how permissive the peer is.

3. **A peer request is a delegation edge, even though no credential crosses it.** This is the only part that requires new machinery. The transport that carries agent-to-agent messages must attach and propagate the chain, because nothing in the message itself will.

4. **A chain whose most recent human principal is not the immediate requester loses the human-only capability set entirely.** Actions gated on human authorization — a purchase, a message sent under the user's name, an irreversible deletion — require that the human be the immediate requester, not merely somewhere upstream. Human authority does not survive an agent hop.

Two implementation notes decide whether this works. First, the enforcement point is the transport and the tool gate, not the model. A model asked to reason about its own provenance is being asked to enforce policy on data it cannot verify. Second, the chain must be harness-authored. If agent A can write its own provenance into the text it sends, the provenance is worth nothing; this is the same reason data-flow defenses derive control flow from the trusted query rather than from model output, and the lesson transfers directly.

The cost is real and I would rather state it than have it discovered. Intersection makes multi-agent systems less capable by construction, and it will break workflows that everyone involved considers legitimate: a planner delegating a task to a specialist that is better equipped for it now inherits the planner's narrowest constraints. That is the trade. It is the same trade capability systems made, and it is why they are safe to compose.

What I have built is one hook on one laptop: a gate that stamps a chain onto messages crossing an agent bridge and refuses actions whose intersection is empty. It is enough to demonstrate that the enforcement point exists and that the rule is implementable at the transport. It is not a platform, and one machine is not an evaluation.

The contribution I would like to survive is smaller than an implementation and, I think, more useful. Restrictions are enforced per agent. Capabilities compose per system. We have known the fix since at least 1999 and cannot apply it, because a sentence sent from one agent to another is a delegation, and no system we have built treats it as one.

### Lente: presa

Draft 1 is the one to publish. It is the only draft where the novel claim is isolated cleanly enough to survive a hostile read: the distinction from the confused deputy lands by paragraph three ("Its policy engine returns the correct answer to the question it was asked, and the question nobody asked is who is upstream of the request"), the prior art is conceded rather than obscured ("None of that is my contribution"), the single study is not overclaimed ("One study does not establish that refusal quality is anti-correlated with amplification, and I will not claim that it does"), and the cost is stated before a reviewer can discover it ("Intersection makes multi-agent systems less capable by construction"). That combination is what makes an opinion piece survive contact with the field it is addressing. Two things in it are load-bearing and appear nowhere else: the enforcement boundary is the vendor, the capability boundary is the machine, and the gap between them is nobody's artifact; and the reason the 1999 fix cannot be applied is that no credential crosses an agent-to-agent request, so there is no edge for a policy engine to reduce. That second point is the piece. It answers the "prompt injection again" test — this is not an attack, nothing is deceived, and the failure is visible in a default configuration.

Draft 0 places second and is the better-organized argument for the narrower readership; its three clauses and its no-provider finding are the strongest policy material in the set, which is why four of the seven grafts come from it. Draft 2 places third despite having the best prose in the set. Its opening is warm but slow — a hundred words of scene before anything happens — and its rule arrives as a paragraph of prose rather than clauses, which fails the quotability test an acquiring editor applies to a policy piece.
